# Turn gaming PCs into a people's datacenter

**As of November 2026, nobody has shipped the full "people's datacenter".** Pluralis has proved the training half: it pretrained an 8.6B model on 500B tokens over the internet on consumer-class GPUs, about 60 contributor nodes at a time. It has shipped none of the people's half. Contributors earn only FLOP "points", the weights are unreleased, and the client needs 24 GB of VRAM, about 80 GB of RAM and a North-American connection. Nobody else combines consumer hardware, one shared model trained from scratch, pay, and an ownership or inference right. The vision is open, and the open parts are the ones that need legal and economic design, not breakthrough algorithms. These parts are the ownership instrument, contributor inference rights, a household hardware tier of 12–16 GB cards on asymmetric home broadband, an EU swarm, and verified pay. It is technically feasible on a phased ladder. A 1–1.5B dense model trained with DiLoCo/SparseLoCo-style full replicas can run on 100–200 NVIDIA gaming GPUs today, at about 2–3.5×10²² FLOP over three months (estimate). About 1,000 sustained nodes could reach a SmolLM3-class 3B model (about 2×10²³ FLOP), and 5–10k nodes a roughly 30B-total / 3B-active mixture-of-experts that members can run at home. No consumer network has yet sustained more than about 60–100 concurrent nodes on one model run, so each rung is a bet. Paid at "electricity plus" rates, consumer FLOPs cost about the same as rented H100s. The project's justification is ownership, legitimacy and local-first inference, not cheap compute. The plan that fits a 1–2 person part-time team in France has four steps. Start with a fine-tune and RL pilot under a loi-1901 association. Run a 1.5B from-scratch MVP paid from a €30–50k grant pool (estimate). Convert to a multi-stakeholder cooperative (SCIC) before scaling. Issue no profit-bearing token at any stage. Build on Apache/MIT code (Pluralis Agora, Templar's SparseLoCo, torchft) rather than competing with it.

This report re-centres the earlier AIBC research on the owner's actual goal. Those reports ranked collaborative pretraining last (47/100) because they assumed Covenant-72B's 8×B200-per-peer hardware and optimized for a quick, publishable "verifiable autoresearch" market. Once the goal is a people's model and the household tier is costed properly, that direction moves from last place to the only one that serves the mission (re-scored below at about 61/100). Most of the earlier work still applies: the verification analysis, the contract audit, the no-token EU legal stance, the funding stack, the cost tables and the kill-gate discipline. One earlier claim needs correcting. The revival report described Pluralis-8B as "330 mostly consumer-GPU nodes". That figure counts nodes over the whole run, not households in parallel (details in the first section).

## Pluralis proved the training half; nobody has shipped the people's half

### The corrected Pluralis-8B record shows prosumer boxes, not living-room PCs

The Pluralis Agora report (July 2026) is the most important primary source for this question, and its fine print changes how it should be read. The run trained an 8.6B model, about 12B "dense-equivalent", on 500B FineWeb-Edu tokens over 40 days. It reached **63% of a centralized H100 baseline's efficiency** at about 3.08×10²² FLOP ([Agora, arXiv 2607.13332](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.13332)). A 6·N·D check agrees: 6 × 8.6×10⁹ × 5×10¹¹ ≈ 2.6×10²², which rises to about 3.6×10²² on the 12B dense-equivalent count. Admissions were **capped at 60 concurrent contributor nodes**, with a live average of about 61 and a peak of 63. Over the run there were 330 contributor nodes from 176 unique operators, with 669 joins and 607 departures. The fleet was **55% RTX 4090/5090 and 45% L40S, RTX 6000 Ada or RTX PRO 6000** workstation and datacenter cards. **Median host RAM was 504 GB**, ranging from 80 GB to 2 TB, and **median download bandwidth was 703 Mbit/s**. The RTX 4090's share fell from about 32% to about 9% during the run, while 5090s and L40S cards grew. The first and last pipeline stages ran on Pluralis-owned cloud nodes, as did the first 5,000 steps. Contributors held only the middle "body" stages, which reached 18.6–22.4% MFU ([arXiv 2607.13332](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.13332)).

What this proves is that consumer-class 24–32 GB cards can carry real pretraining at 8B when the operator holds the fragile parts. It does not prove that households with 12–16 GB cards on 20 Mbit/s uplinks can take part. Pluralis's current requirements make the gap explicit: at least 24 GB of VRAM and 70 GB of RAM per GPU, 200 Mbps down and 100 Mbps up (speed-tested at join), under 80 ms RTT to North-American peers ("a North American instance is effectively required"), an inbound port forwarded on the router, and no macOS ([Pluralis requirements](https://pluralis.ai/docs/quick-start/requirements/)). Demand is not the bottleneck. **612 would-be clients competed for 60 slots** and the median wait was 5.8 hours ([arXiv 2607.13332](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.13332)), all with no pay at all.

The economic layer is missing entirely. Pluralis's thesis promises "partial model ownership" and "programmatic value flow from downstream use" through weights that no participant can extract ([Pluralis, A Third Path](https://pluralis.ai/blog/a-third-path-protocol-learning/)). The docs, however, say "No financial incentive yet. Points reflect contribution only" ([Pluralis Points](https://pluralis.ai/docs/quick-start/points/)). No Pluralis model appears on Hugging Face ([HF API](https://huggingface.co/api/models?author=PluralisResearch)). A later 13B "systems test" reports 61% protocol overhead and 14.9% mean MFU, and no run was live on 2026-11-05 ([agora.pluralis.ai](https://agora.pluralis.ai/)). Pluralis's $7.6M seed reportedly came with warrants for a future token ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/crypto/2025/03/19/pluralis-research-7-6-million-openai-coinfund-union-square-ventures)), so an economic launch could come at any time. Pluralis is therefore at "technical proof at 8B, economics to be decided".

### Everyone else holds a different fragment of the vision

Macrocosmos IOTA (Bittensor SN9) has the most consumer-friendly product. A one-click Mac app opened to everyone on 2026-02-10, needs 16 GB of RAM, and pays Bittensor emissions for layer-sharded training of a shared model ([Macrocosmos](https://macrocosmosai.substack.com/p/welcome-to-train-at-home)). Its demonstrated consumer run is small: 1.5B parameters, 13 days and **815 participants**, a self-reported figure ([IOTA on X](https://x.com/IOTA_SN9/status/2025987505473085896)). Its 100B Orion run used 48 datacenter A100s ([Macrocosmos](https://macrocosmosai.substack.com/p/orion-100b-distributed-pretraining)), and its newest public model is a Qwen3.5-4B fine-tune trained on four A6000s ([HF](https://huggingface.co/macrocosm-os/iota-decide-4b)). Contributors get emissions, not ownership or inference rights.

Nous Psyche is permissioned and runs on Solana devnet with no token rewards. Its Consilience-40B pretraining has had no checkpoint since August 2025, and the network now does Hermes post-training ([ownyourmind review](https://ownyourmind.ai/projects/nous-research/)). The well-funded players pulled back from live consumer networks in 2026. Prime Intellect archived its protocol on 2026-01-27 ([GitHub](https://github.com/PrimeIntellect-ai/protocol)) and raised $130M for enterprise agents ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/08/prime-intellect-raises-130m-series-a-to-help-enterprises-build-their-own-ai-agents/)). Gensyn lists RL Swarm as legacy ([Gensyn docs](https://docs.gensyn.ai/testnet)). Templar's Covenant-72B needed 8×B200 per peer before the team left Bittensor ([arXiv 2603.08163](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.08163v2)). Several other projects show that one piece works but not the whole. Grass has about 8.5M registered users by its own count, which shows consumers will install a passive app for rewards, but it trains nothing collectively ([dextools](https://www.dextools.io/news/what-is-grass-crypto-depin-bandwidth-ai-training-guide-2026)). Apertus 1.5 (Apache-2.0, July 2026) shows Europe will fund a public-good model, but citizens contribute no compute and gain no rights ([HF API](https://huggingface.co/api/models/swiss-ai/Apertus-v1.5-70B)). The research found **no operating member-owned cooperative training an LLM on pooled member compute**, only data co-ops and proposals ([Hypha](https://hypha.coop/dripline/the-other-ai/)).

The table reads as a map of what is still open. Nobody has shipped five things, ranked here by how defensible each would be for AIBC. The first is a legally real ownership and remuneration instrument. The second is contributor inference rights defined as a right rather than a promise. The third is a genuine household tier: 12–16 GB NVIDIA cards first, then AMD and Apple, on 10–50 Mbit/s uplinks. The fourth is an EU-latency swarm. The fifth is verified pay in a setting with hundreds of untrusted nodes. The first and fourth are where a French team has a natural edge. The intellectual framing of "collectively owned, unextractable protocol models" already belongs to Pluralis, so a pitch built on that thesis alone will read as derivative. Differentiation has to come from what Pluralis has not shipped.

## Gaming GPUs can train a 1.5B model now and a 3B-active MoE later

### Full-replica DiLoCo fits home links; pipelines need prosumer links

Only two families of methods have been shown over the internet above 1B parameters. The first is data-parallel DiLoCo and its compressed descendants, where every node holds a full replica. The second is pipeline parallelism with compressed activations, which is what Agora does. Covenant-72B shows the first works with SparseLoCo: more than **146× compression**, about 70 seconds of communication per 20-minute round on 110 Mb/s uplinks, and 94.5% compute utilization ([arXiv 2603.08163](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.08163v2)). Agora shows the second works, but its own estimate puts same-stage replica sync at about 113 Mbit/s with BF16 all-reduce, which is already above typical home uplinks. Pipelining also needs peer-to-peer activation traffic, so it needs inbound connectivity ([arXiv 2607.13332](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.13332)). FSDP, tensor parallelism and expert-parallel all-to-all over a WAN are ruled out. All-gathering a 7B model's 14 GB of BF16 weights every step would take about 19 minutes per step at 100 Mbit/s (estimate).

Memory arithmetic sets the model size for a full replica (estimate). Mixed-precision AdamW needs about 16 bytes per parameter. A 24 GB card therefore holds about a 1–1.2B model fully on the GPU after activations. With the optimizer and master weights offloaded to CPU RAM, as Agora does, the GPU needs only about 4 bytes per parameter. A 12 GB card then reaches about 2B and a 24 GB card about 4B, but the host needs about 12 bytes per parameter: 36 GB of RAM for a 3B model. LLMQ, an 8-bit CUDA trainer, fits a 7B model on one 16 GB gaming card at about 50% FLOP utilization, but it is a single-node system ([arXiv 2512.15306](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15306)). The MuLoCo variant uses Muon inner steps, which keep one momentum buffer instead of Adam's two. That saves about 4 bytes per parameter of offloaded state, which matters for gaming PCs with 32 GB of RAM.

Bandwidth sets the ceiling (estimate). A 1.5B model's dense BF16 pseudo-gradient is about 3 GB, so at about 146× compression a node uploads about 20 MB per round, roughly 16 seconds at 10 Mbit/s. **Download is what binds.** Merging sparse updates from many peers approaches dense density, so each node pulls about 1–3 GB per round, which costs 5–15% overhead on a 100 Mbit/s line with 20–30 minute rounds. At 7B the download grows to about 6–14 GB per round and full replicas stop working for typical homes. This is the main technical reason the plan below stays at 1.5–3B dense before switching to independently trained experts.

The same arithmetic exposes an infrastructure cost that is easy to miss (estimate). With 150 nodes each pulling about 2 GB three times an hour, the coordinator serves about 20 TB a day. Storage with egress included, which Hugging Face Buckets list at $12/TB/month ([HF Storage](https://huggingface.co/storage)), or peer or regional redistribution, is a design requirement, not an optimization. Longer rounds also cut this traffic in proportion.

Heterogeneity is manageable, but the cross-vendor case has not been demonstrated. Agora's 1B ablations tolerate **10:1 speed differences** and degrade only when all-reduce participation drops below about 85% ([arXiv 2607.13332](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.13332)). Asynchronous variants help further: HeLoCo reports up to +22.1% over synchronous DiLoCo ([arXiv 2606.00271](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.00271v1)), and the Delayed-Nesterov plus Dynamic-Local-Updates recipe "consistently excels across all heterogeneity levels" ([arXiv 2401.09135](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.09135)). DeepMind's Decoupled DiLoCo replaces the lock-step barrier with a quorum and a grace window that merges whatever arrives, weighted by tokens ([arXiv 2604.21428](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.21428)).

The gaps are precision and vendors. FP8 exists only on RTX 40 and 50 cards, and BF16 from RTX 30 onward ([Forgather FP8](https://forgather.readthedocs.io/en/latest/trainers/fp8-training/)). **No public run has co-trained one model across NVIDIA, AMD and Apple.** Cross-vendor work exists only as Mac rollouts feeding an NVIDIA trainer ([Pluralis](https://pluralis.ai/blog/rl-post-training-on-macs/)) and as DiLoCo on datacenter AMD MI300 ([ROCm blog](https://rocm.blogs.amd.com/artificial-intelligence/decentralized-training/README.html)).

Because DiLoCo sends only compressed pseudo-gradients and never uses NCCL collectives, it is vendor-neutral in principle. The MVP should still standardize on BF16 for the exchanged state, let each node choose its inner-step precision, and treat Macs and Radeons as later additions that need validation.

### Six months of contributor time buys a 3B model per thousand nodes

All figures in the next table are estimates built from sourced inputs. An RTX 4090 peaks at about **165 dense BF16 TFLOPS** with FP32 accumulation ([aiwiki](https://aiwiki.ai/wiki/nvidia_geforce_rtx_4090/raw)). Agora's contributor stages achieved about 20% MFU, and volunteers are online only part of the day. Three scenarios combine MFU while online with the fraction of time online:

| Conservative | 20% | 40% | about 13 TFLOPS | 2.1×10²⁰ |
| Middle | 30% | 60% | about 30 TFLOPS | 4.7×10²⁰ |
| Optimistic | 45% | 80% | about 59 TFLOPS | 9.4×10²⁰ |

A further 0.5–0.65 penalty converts raw FLOP into what a centralized cluster would need for the same result. Agora measured 63%, and an MIRI simulation estimates about one-third at 100 Mbit/s for very large runs ([arXiv 2605.29359](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29359)). Sizes below follow 6·N·D, so 10²³ FLOP buys an 8B model on about 2T tokens, or a 1B model on about 16T tokens. It is not "roughly a 1B model" as one note phrased it.

The table carries three honest caveats. First, "SOTA-for-its-class" realistically means a 1–4B dense model or a small-active MoE that matches open models of the same size from 12–18 months earlier. The small-model bar moves fast: Qwen3-4B was trained on 36T tokens ([Qwen3 report](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.09388v1)). Second, the 1,000-node row is **15–20 times beyond the largest concurrent consumer fleet anyone has run**, about 60 nodes for Agora and 815 self-reported participants in IOTA's small run. Third, donated electricity is large in aggregate. At 60% online time a 4090 PC burns about 240 kWh a month, so 10,000 nodes consume about €4–9M of power a year (estimate). The incentive design has to cover that.

### Train dense first, then grow experts that members can run

For the MVP, a small dense decoder is the only architecture where a full replica fits on each node: about 1.2–1.5B parameters, GQA, a vocabulary of 65k tokens or fewer, 2–4k context and BF16 master weights. The small vocabulary matters. A 150k vocabulary at width 2,048 spends 0.3B parameters on embeddings alone (estimate).

For the scale-up, one design serves both training and inference: a sparse MoE with about 3B active parameters. A 30B-A3B model at 4 bits occupies about 16–18 GB and runs at small-model speed on a 24 GB GPU or a 32 GB Mac (estimate). Training such a model jointly needs expert-parallel all-to-all, which fails over a WAN. The workable route is to **grow it from independently trained experts**. Branch-Train-MiX branches a dense seed, trains domain experts "in an embarrassingly parallel fashion", and merges them as MoE experts with a fine-tuned router ([arXiv 2403.07816](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07816)). FlexOlmo trains experts against a frozen public anchor and combines them without joint training, up to 37B total / 20B active, and beats a standard MoE at equal FLOPs ([arXiv 2507.07024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07024)).

Mapped onto the network, each expert becomes a cohort of 50–500 nodes running its own DiLoCo loop. Cohorts exchange only their own branch's deltas, and cross-cohort communication happens once, at merge (estimate). Neither BTX nor FlexOlmo has been run by volunteers or over a WAN, so this step is an engineering bet, not a demonstrated recipe.

Two more training-time choices pay off at inference. A short quantization-aware phase restores int4 quality cheaply: Gemma 3 needed about 5,000 QAT steps ([Gemma 3 report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.19786)). Native ternary training (BitNet) is proven only at 2B on 4T tokens and does not reduce training memory ([Azure AI Labs](https://labs.ai.azure.com/?p=900)), so BF16 or FP8 pretraining with late QAT is the safer path. Nested training (MatFormer, Nemotron Elastic) yields 6B and 9B submodels inside a 12B parent from one run ([arXiv 2511.16664](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.16664)), so one checkpoint can serve every contributor tier. That has not yet been shown in a low-communication setting.

Fine-tuning and RL are much easier and already proven on consumer hardware, which makes them the right first public milestone. QLoRA fits an 8B model in about 6 GB and a 32B model in about 26 GB ([Unsloth](https://unsloth.ai/docs/get-started/beginner-start-here/unsloth-requirements)). Pluralis's Stoa had 14 Macs on home internet generate all the rollouts for an 8.3B MoE, with median deltas of 82 MB against a 9 GB checkpoint ([Pluralis](https://pluralis.ai/blog/rl-post-training-on-macs/)). RL rollouts need only inference memory, so 8 GB RTX 3060/4060 cards that are marginal for pretraining become useful there.

### The client must be outbound-only, idle-aware and churn-proof

The current state-of-the-art client is Agora's. It runs only on Linux or WSL2, needs a forwarded inbound port and ships a closed integrity binary, which is far from one-click for gamers ([Pluralis requirements](https://pluralis.ai/docs/quick-start/requirements/)). The friendlier template is Stoa's: outbound HTTPS only, object storage, and workers that can join, leave or crash without stalling the run ([Pluralis](https://pluralis.ai/blog/rl-post-training-on-macs/)). Covenant exchanged pseudo-gradients the same way, through object storage ([arXiv 2603.08163](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.08163v2)).

Churn will be heavy. Agora's 669 joins and 607 departures over 40 days imply sessions of a few days (estimate). BOINC hosts last about 91 days on average ([Anderson & Fedak](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0602061)), and by 2010 about five in six registered BOINC volunteers had stopped ([Darch & Carusi](https://www.dhi.ac.uk/san/waysofbeing/data/health-jones-darch-2010.pdf)).

The client design follows from this. Ship a Windows/WSL2 installer and a Linux container with a pinned CUDA build. FP8 kernels need sm_89 explicitly, which some PyTorch builds omit ([PyTorch forum](https://discuss.pytorch.org/t/sm-89-not-listed-in-the-torch-cuda-get-arch-list/215827)). Offer a power-limit slider and "only when idle" scheduling. Use 15–30 minute rounds, so a departing node loses at most one round of its own work and the global step never waits for it.

## Pay for verified tokens, not for claimed FLOPs

### Consumer GPUs offer no hardware trust, so output checks must do the work

Consumer GeForce cards have no confidential-computing or attestation support. NVIDIA CC covers Hopper and Blackwell datacenter parts ([NVIDIA](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/confidential-computing-on-h100-gpus-for-secure-and-trustworthy-ai/)), and even those TEEs fall to a DDR5 interposer costing under $1,000 ([TEE.fail](https://tee.fail/)). Agora's "unmodified build" check is a compiled binary that computes a hash on the contributor's own machine ([agora repo](https://github.com/PluralisResearch/agora)). That is obfuscation, not proof, and Pluralis's docs state that adversarial-peer defense "is not yet implemented" ([Agora docs](https://pluralis.ai/docs/agora-system/overview/)). Psyche's verifier and tie-breaker committee roles are literally `todo!()` in its March 2026 code ([psyche repo](https://github.com/PsycheFoundation/psyche)). Zero-knowledge proofs of training stop around 10M parameters, as the earlier AIBC reports documented ([arXiv 2307.16273](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.16273)). Correctness therefore has to come from checking outputs, with economic deterrence behind it.

The threat model is sharper than "cheaters". If the coordinator owns a curated public corpus and assigns shards deterministically from a seed, as Templar, Psyche and Agora all do, contributors cannot inject documents. They can only craft updates that behave like poisoned data. That still matters, because **about 250 poisoned documents backdoor models from 600M to 13B parameters**, a count nearly independent of model size ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/small-samples-poison)). Loss-delta scoring on clean held-out data will not see a backdoor that only fires on a trigger. Hence the strongest defense is random replay: compare what a node submitted with what its assigned data would have produced.

### A five-layer stack costs about 5–15% of compute

For the DiLoCo MVP, the deployed starting point is Templar's Gauntlet (MIT). A validator applies a peer's pseudo-gradient and measures the loss reduction on that peer's assigned data against random data. A peer that helps more on random data than on its own assignment scores negative, which deters copying. Gauntlet adds liveness and sync checks, OpenSkill ranking, and normalization to the median norm ([arXiv 2603.08163](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.08163v2)). The median-norm step matters: a secondary account describes a "huge values" attack that disrupted the subnet for about 48 hours ([learnbittensor](https://learnbittensor.org/subnets/3)).

Two changes adapt Gauntlet to consumers. First, accept and weight every update that passes, instead of Covenant's top-20 per round, which would waste honest compute when hundreds of small peers take part. Second, add **sampled replay**. Because the starting weights, data shard and seed of every round are known, an operator auditor can re-run a randomly chosen node's whole round. The auditor then compares the resulting pseudo-gradient under a tolerance calibrated from honest replays across GPU models. That is the method of OVIG, which reports 0% attack success at +14.3% compute ([arXiv 2606.21045](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21045)), and of TAO, which calibrated tolerances across A100, H100, RTX 6000 and RTX 4090 at 0.3% overhead for inference ([arXiv 2510.16028](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.16028)).

Neither OVIG nor TAO has released code, and nobody has published tolerances for BF16 training on GeForce cards. **Whether a 30–100-step inner loop stays within a usable tolerance across a 3090, a 4090 and a 5090 is the single most important experiment for AIBC to run first.** It is also the natural successor to the earlier reports' noise-floor work.

Sampling rates can follow BOINC's adaptive replication. New hosts are checked heavily (for example 25%) until they have 10 consecutive clean results, and the rate then decays toward about 5% ([BOINC wiki](https://github.com/BOINC/boinc/wiki/Adaptive-Replication)). At a 5% replay rate, a node that cheats on 100 rounds is caught with 1 − 0.95¹⁰⁰ ≈ 99.4% probability (calculation). A CEA-LIST result supports random scheduling plus about 10% lazy verification against backdoors, cutting attack success from 99.5% to 7.2%, though only in a toy single-trainer setting ([arXiv 2607.06643](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06643)).

A later "pro tier" running Agora-style pipelines would add other layers. SENTINEL's cheap EMA statistics on inter-stage activations score 80–100% F1 on most attacks, but **0% on some gradient attacks** and 68.6% recall at 4B, with no code ([arXiv 2603.03592](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03592)). It would be combined with redundant execution of 5–10% of microbatches, operator-held first and last stages, and coordinator-assigned stages.

| Layer | What it stops | Cost (published or estimated) |
|---|---|---|
| Admission, signed short-lived credentials, coordinator-assigned shards and stages | Unauthorized peers; choosing one's own data or stage | Negligible (Agora Authorizer) |
| Median-norm clipping plus robust averaging | "Huge value" updates; crude Byzantine updates | Small |
| Gauntlet-style assigned-vs-random loss scoring | Free-riding, copying, useless updates | One evaluation on a subset of peers per round |
| Adaptive sampled replay under calibrated tolerance | Faked work, lazy shortcuts, many crafted updates | ≈ replay rate (5–25%) of node compute, estimate |
| Escrow of 14–30 days of earnings, forfeited on proven cheating | Hit-and-run attacks | Bookkeeping |
| Operator-held ends and SENTINEL statistics (pipeline tier only) | Corrupted activations and gradients | No duplicated compute; overhead unmeasured |

The security note estimates total steady-state overhead at about **5–15% of compute** (estimate). That compares with more than 100% for full replication and about 10⁴× for zero-knowledge proofs. Three residual risks should be stated in any pitch. Trigger-conditional backdoors are bounded only probabilistically. Two payees colluding inside a replay pair could cover for each other, so the coordinator should pick pairs across payees. And cross-GPU BF16 tolerances are not yet characterized.

### Credit is computed server-side, per accepted unit of work

Every live or recent consumer project pays for compute or presence, not utility, and most trust the node's own report. Agora's points equal "the raw pflops it has processed" plus 1 PFLOP of "presence" per active hour, reported by the node ([agora repo](https://github.com/PluralisResearch/agora)). Psyche splits a pool equally among clients that finish an epoch ([psyche repo](https://github.com/PsycheFoundation/psyche)). Twenty years of SETI@home and BOINC show that self-reported or benchmark-claimed credit gets inflated by modified clients. BOINC's fix was server-computed credit, normalized per host and averaged across replicas ([BOINC CreditNew](https://github.com/BOINC/boinc/wiki/CreditNew)). Folding@home sets points per work unit on a benchmark machine and pays a return-speed bonus only to reliable hosts ([F@H](https://foldinghome-client-documentation.readthedocs.io/en/latest/points_overview.html)). Shapley-style attribution of usefulness is "prohibitively expensive" at this scale ([arXiv 2505.23246](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23246v1)).

The rule for AIBC follows from that record. **Credit = tokens processed in an accepted update × the analytic FLOPs per token for the model** (about 6·N). The server computes it, and a bounded quality multiplier from the loss score (for example 0.5–1.5) adjusts it, without winner-take-all (design proposal). A 3060 then earns less than a 5090 without anyone trusting a benchmark claim. Presence pay is either zero or a tiny stipend gated by a liveness challenge, because presence is the easiest thing to farm.

Identity is mostly a payout problem in the EU. Anyone paid in euros or through an exchange is KYC'd by the payout rail anyway, and DAC8 has required crypto service providers to collect tax identity since 2026-01-01 ([Grant Thornton](https://www.grantthornton.nl/en/insights-en/articles/dac8-reporting-requirements-for-crypto-and-digital-asset-service-providers-in-the-eu/)). The rule "one verified payee, many nodes" gives Sybil resistance for caps and bonuses, and the project stores only a pseudonymous payee token. EUDI Wallet attestations become available from member states around late 2026, with private-sector acceptance about a year later ([fintech.global](https://fintech.global/2026/07/13/eidas-2-0-deadlines-loom-two-tracks-one-trust-chain/)). Biometric proof-of-personhood is a poor fit: the Bavarian DPA ordered World to delete iris data in December 2024 ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/331713/german-authority-orders-eyeball-scanning-world-project-to-delete-data-after-investigation)).

The volunteer also needs protecting from the operator's software. The NVIDIAScape container escape (CVE-2025-23266, CVSS 9.0) took full host control through a three-line Dockerfile until Container Toolkit 1.17.8 fixed it ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2025/07/critical-nvidia-container-toolkit-flaw.html)). The client should run only signed, operator-built images and never execute third-party code. It should load only safetensors, use rootless containers with gVisor's nvproxy where compatible ([gVisor](https://gvisor.dev/docs/user_guide/gpu)), and publish its source.

## Electricity-plus pay, cooperative ownership, no profit token

### A contributor's honest margin is tens of euros a month

At late-2026 tariffs, a 4090 PC drawing about 0.55 kW at the wall costs about **€0.11 an hour in France**, using the regulated EDF Base tariff of €0.2001/kWh from August 2026 ([Kelwatt](https://www.kelwatt.fr/actu/tarif-edf-reglemente-1er-aout-2026)). The same PC costs about €0.20 an hour in Germany, at €0.3618/kWh, and about €0.16 at the EU average of €0.2832/kWh ([Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Electricity_price_statistics)). The per-hour figures are estimates. The rental market pays less than outsiders assume. Vast.ai's 4090 medians run about $0.39–0.52 an hour gross ([Activepieces](https://www.activepieces.com/blog/vast-ai-gpu-pricing-per-hour-in-2026-and-extra-fees.md)), and SaladCloud's batch tier is $0.16 ([Spheron](https://www.spheron.network/blog/saladcloud-pricing-2026-gpu-cost-vs-spheron/)). After platform fees and idle time, renting out a card is net-positive only where power is cheap.

A people's model should therefore pay a country-indexed floor of about **€0.12–0.25 per verified 4090-hour**, roughly electricity × 1.3–1.5 (estimate). Run 60% of the time, a 4090 earns about €52–108 a month against about €48 of French electricity, a net of a few euros to about €60 (estimate). This is cost recovery plus a margin, not passive income, and the pitch must say so.

The same rates fix what training costs (estimate). At about 20% realized MFU, a 4090 delivers about 1.2×10¹⁷ FLOP an hour. The **1.5B MVP** needs about 194,000 node-hours: 150 nodes for three months at 60% online time. That is **€29–49k in payouts**. A 10²³ FLOP run needs about 0.85M 4090-hours, about €125–210k and 470 MWh. A 10²⁴ run costs about €1.3–2.1M, and 10²⁵ is out of reach at €13–21M. For comparison, 10²³ FLOP on H100s at about $2 an hour and 40% MFU costs about $140k. The whole MVP's compute could be rented for roughly $30k.

**Consumer GPUs are cost-competitive only when paid near electricity cost.** The project's case rests on ownership, legitimacy and the inference benefit, not on cheaper FLOPs. It also means the MVP's 1.5B model is worth little on its own. What the MVP buys is a proven network, a payout pipeline, a legal shell and a community.

### Token emissions failed; stable pay plus membership did not

The 2024–2026 record is unambiguous. IO trades about 96% below its peak ([Solana Compass](https://solanacompass.com/projects/ionet)). Helium's market cap fell about 81% year on year despite revenue growth ([Sarson Funds](https://sarsonfunds.com/depins-leading-edge-why-heliums-growth-metrics-demand-attention/)). Bittensor's subsidies reportedly outpace organic revenue ([KuCoin](https://www.kucoin.com/news/flash/bittensor-faces-valuation-risk-as-52m-subsidies-outpace-organic-revenue)). The survivors moved to stable-value pay. Grass's second season pays rewards in USDC ([hokanews](https://www.hokanews.com/2026/07/grass-reveals-season-2-claim-date-as.html)), and io.net pays suppliers USD-stable amounts backed by reserves ([CoinDesk](https://coindesk.com/research/the-incentive-dynamic-engine-a-new-era-for-io-net-tokenomics)).

EU law reinforces that direction. Under ESMA's guidelines, a token carrying profit rights is likely a MiFID II transferable security, with prospectus and intermediary duties a 1–2 person team cannot carry ([BDO Malta](https://bdo.com.mt/en-gb/news/news-in-2025/understanding-the-esma-guidelines-on-crypto-assets-as-financial-instruments)). The earlier AIBC research already established the rest. Points, valueless test tokens and payouts in an existing stablecoin or in fiat stay outside MiCA. An issued token needs a white paper unless the Art. 4(3) exemption holds, and requiring KYC or signalling a listing erodes that exemption ([ESMA MiCA Art. 4](https://www.esma.europa.eu/publications-and-data/interactive-single-rulebook/mica/article-4-offers-public-crypto-assets-other)). The earlier notes estimated MiCA counsel and a white paper at €20–60k (unsourced estimate).

The design that survives has three layers. The first is stable euro or stablecoin pay per verified unit of work, funded by grants and later by revenue. The second is a non-transferable record of contributions that converts into membership, votes and inference priority. The third is optional upside through cooperative patronage rebates, never through a tradable profit token. Volunteers alone can carry a mission, but with heavy churn.

### An association first, a multi-stakeholder cooperative at scale

*The following points are not legal advice and need confirmation by French counsel or an expert-comptable before any real payout.*

The simplest MVP vehicle is a **French association loi 1901**. It is cheap to declare, eligible for grants and member-governed, and it can own the weights and trademark. Paying members flat amounts risks requalification as salary, so payouts should be documented expense reimbursements or contracts for compute services. That point is uncertain under French law.

At scale, the vehicle that best fits "by the people, for the people" is a **SCIC**, a multi-stakeholder cooperative company. Contributors, users, founders, partner organizations and public bodies sit in separate voting colleges. There must be at least three colleges and no college may hold a majority alone. **At least 57.5% of annual surplus goes to indivisible reserves**, which would fund the next training run ([Avise](https://www.avise.org/sites/default/files/atoms/files/20150105/201412_avise_scic.pdf); [Bpifrance Création](https://bpifrance-creation.fr/pid804/scic.html?espace=1)).

Contributors would buy one €10–50 share that cannot be transferred and is redeemable at par, so it avoids securities law. The remaining surplus could fund capped share interest or patronage-style rebates proportional to verified GPU-hours. Nobody has yet used SCIC rebates to pay compute providers, so counsel must confirm the mechanics. Alternatives are weaker. A Wyoming or Marshall Islands DAO LLC adds a foreign entity with little case law ([Legal Nodes](https://legalnodes.com/article/wyoming-dao-llc)). A Swiss association or foundation is the fallback only if a token track ever becomes necessary.

Paying contributors has tax consequences for them. Individuals in France would declare the payouts as income. The micro-BNC ceiling for 2026 is €77,700 with a 34% allowance ([Finalib](https://finalib.fr/blog/fiscalite/micro-bic-micro-bnc-seuils-obligations-2026)). GPU rental may instead fall under BIC "location de matériel"; this is unresolved and a rescrit fiscal would settle it. Paying in USDC creates a crypto taxable event on conversion. A monthly cap of about €150 per person limits fraud exposure and keeps amounts small (design proposal).

Avoiding quotas or imposed schedules keeps the relationship clearly outside employment. Under GDPR, contributor identity, IPs, telemetry and wallet addresses are personal data, so identities stay off-chain and only Merkle roots go on-chain. The ToS also falls under unfair-terms rules, which cover clauses such as unilateral rate changes.

The AI Act shapes the licence. A model is indicatively a general-purpose AI (GPAI) model above **10²³ training FLOP**. Full enforcement, including fines, began on 2026-08-02. The open-source exemption waives only the documentation duties, and only without monetisation; the copyright policy and the public training-data summary always remain ([EC GPAI FAQ](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/guidelines-obligations-gpai-providers)).

Two consequences follow. The 1.5B MVP, at about 2–3.5×10²² FLOP, sits below the indicative threshold. So does a fine-tune of Apertus, because a modifier becomes a provider only above one-third of the original compute ([Stibbe](https://www.stibbe.com/publications-and-insights/the-guidelines-for-providers-of-general-purpose-ai-models-are-here-the)). The 3B phase crosses the threshold. If it is released under Apache-2.0 with no paid access, the open-source exemption applies. A members-only embargo or a paid API probably counts as monetisation and triggers full Art. 53 documentation, estimated at a few person-weeks per release.

| Package | Entity | Pay | Upside and rights | Fits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Open Commons MVP | Association loi 1901 | €0.10–0.25 per verified 4090-hour, country-indexed, capped per person, from a grant | Non-transferable points; first access to checkpoints; free inference quota; name in the model card; no promise of a token | Phases 0–1 |
| B. People's Model Cooperative | SCIC SAS with ≥4 colleges (contributors 30–40% of votes) | Same, funded by grants plus revenue | Member share; patronage rebate; inference priority; votes on release and safety policy | Phase 2 onward |
| C. Token track (reserve) | Swiss entity or a regulated partner | MiCA-exempt useful-work token plus a stable floor | Liquid but speculative; credibility cost with public funders | Only with a regulated partner |

Money for EU sovereign models flows to consortia and institutions. OpenEuroLLM has €37.4M ([Slator](https://tech.slator.com/enter-openeurollm-open-compliant-diverse-series-of-foundation-models/)), and the Gigafactories call targets more than €30B ([EU Digital Skills](https://digital-skills-jobs.europa.eu/en/latest/news/eu-ai-gigafactories-call-open-until-12-november-targeting-over-eu30-billion-investment)). A small team cannot lead either. Its realistic sources are four. EuroHPC AI Factories Fast Lane gives up to 50,000 free GPU-hours to AI SMEs, which suits operator-side validators and baselines ([EuroHPC](https://www.eurohpc-ju.europa.eu/ai-factories/ai-factories-access-modes/fast-lane-access-ai-factories_en)). NLnet, whose calls paused after 2026-06-01, is expected to reopen ([NLnet](https://nlnet.nl/news/2026/20260601-call.html)). Current AI-type public-interest funders had $400M at launch ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/02/10/france-tech-companies-and-philanthropies-back-400-million-foundation-to-support-public-interest-ai)). Finally, the Public AI Inference Utility can serve the model ([publicai.co](https://publicai.co/utility)).

The project should be pitched as "citizen-owned compute" that complements public HPC, not as an OpenEuroLLM competitor. A €50–150k grant at €0.15 per hour buys 0.3–1M GPU-hours, enough for a credible 10²²–10²³ FLOP demonstration (estimate).

## Contributors win by running the model at home

### Local inference of an open, tiered family is the real dividend

Inference credits alone are weak compensation. Hosted open models are cheap: gpt-oss-120b costs as little as about $0.15 per million output tokens on OpenRouter ([OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/openai/gpt-oss-120b)). At that price a heavy user consuming 10M tokens a month gets about $1.50 of market value (estimate). The benefit frontier APIs and cheap open APIs cannot match is a strong model that runs privately on hardware the contributor already owns.

That benefit is now achievable. MoE expert offloading keeps attention and the KV cache on the GPU and puts routed experts in system RAM. With it, gpt-oss-120b (117B total, **5.1B active**, native MXFP4, 59 GB) runs at about 47 tokens/s on a single RTX 5090 ([user report](https://x.com/witcheer/status/2062103386615398642); [llama.cpp guide](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/15396)). The governing rule (estimate) is decode speed ≈ memory bandwidth ÷ (active parameters × bytes per parameter). A model with 3–5B active parameters stays fast even with experts in DDR5, while 22–37B-active models drop to about 5–15 tokens/s.

Meanwhile the best open models are drifting out of consumer reach. KTransformers' Qwen3.5 guide lists 4× RTX 4090 and at least 800 GB of RAM as the minimum ([KTransformers](https://github.com/kvcache-ai/ktransformers/blob/main/doc/en/Qwen3.5.md)). Local demand is large: Ollama reports 8.9M monthly developer users, a company figure ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/09/popular-open-source-ai-developer-tool-ollama-raises-65m-grows-to-nearly-9m-users/)).

The design implication is to **train for the contributor fleet's inference envelope**. That means small active parameters, a total size that fits 64–128 GB of RAM at 4 bits, late QAT, and nested sizes that each contributor tier can slice. GGUF and MLX builds should ship on release day with offload presets tuned per hardware profile (estimate):

| Tier | Example hardware | What it runs |
|---|---|---|
| S | 8–12 GB GPU, 16–32 GB RAM | A 2–4B dense nested submodel |
| M | 16–24 GB GPU, 64 GB RAM | A ~30B-A3B MoE |
| L | 32 GB GPU with 128 GB+ RAM, or a large Mac | A ~100–200B-total / 5–10B-active flagship |

### The swarm serves overflow, not chat

Splitting one model across homes over the internet adds capacity, not speed. Petals reached about 6 tokens/s for 70B models ([UPM paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.23464)). prima.cpp manages about 1.5 tokens/s for a 70B model on four home devices ([ICLR 2026](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/e991e5587c1daa49bbf9a818b3f02f9a-Abstract-Conference.html)). Each token crosses every stage, so 20–80 ms per home-to-home hop adds up fast.

The hosted fallback should therefore route each request to one node or LAN cluster that holds a whole replica. Gradient's Parallax scheduler is the current reference design for heterogeneous volunteer GPUs ([arXiv 2509.26182](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.26182)). Integrity is cheap. TOPLOC commits each 32 generated tokens to 258 bytes and validates up to 100× faster than generation ([arXiv 2501.16007](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.16007v1)). Batch-invariant kernels make spot-check replay bitwise reproducible at about 1.6–2× slowdown ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Sep/11/defeating-nondeterminism)). Prompt privacy across untrusted nodes has no solution without TEEs, so local inference remains the only strong privacy guarantee.

Allocation can follow a simple rule (design proposal). Open weights stay free for everyone to run locally, unmetered. On the hosted flagship, contributors get weighted-fair-queue priority proportional to points that decay with about a 90-day half-life, so early contributors cannot lock in priority forever. They also get a capped monthly free quota and a guaranteed minimum tier per verified person. External users pay near market price, and that revenue flows to serving and training contributors by verified work. Multi-LoRA serving lets members train personal or community adapters on the network and run them on one shared base.

### Open weights beat unextractable weights for this mission

Pluralis's Unextractable Protocol Models apply random invertible transforms at stage boundaries, so stolen shards from different times do not fit together. The cost is about 1.6% training time and 3% inference latency ([arXiv 2605.23464](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23464)). They make the network *required* for inference, which is the basis of Pluralis's value-capture thesis. That locks contributors into slow WAN serving and conflicts directly with local-first use. It also forfeits the AI Act open-source exemption and the legitimacy that public funders reward.

The recommended policy is to release the tiered family as open weights under Apache-2.0. The exclusive member benefits are then hosted priority, governance over data and alignment, early access to intermediate checkpoints, and personal adapters. A paid embargo is not one of them, because of the monetisation point above. Value comes from four things in order of defensibility. The model runs well on hardware members already own. It is private and local-first. Its governance is community-owned and its data transparent. It specializes where frontier models are weaker, such as EU languages.

## A four-phase plan with kill gates, from 30 friends to 10,000 nodes

### What changes from the earlier recommendation, and what carries over

The earlier report scored "collaborative pretraining" 47/100, last of seventeen directions. It gave the lowest marks for hardware access and speed to MVP because it assumed Covenant's 8×B200 peers. Re-scoring with the same weights and the evidence above gives about **61/100** (estimate). Hardware access rises to 3 of 5, because full-replica 1–1.5B training fits 12–24 GB cards and Pluralis and IOTA show consumer-class participation. Competition gap rises to 4, because nobody has shipped the ownership, household-tier or EU pieces. Verification stays at 3. Speed, revenue and risk stay low.

On the old rubric the autoresearch flagship (80/100) still wins as a quick paper. It is no longer the right primary objective, because it does not deliver the owner's goal. The autoresearch market becomes an optional side track. Its acceptance-spec and noise-floor methods transfer directly to accepting DiLoCo updates.

Several conclusions carry over unchanged. Proofs and TEEs are not viable for consumers, and Gauntlet-style scoring plus sampled tolerance replay and TOPLOC is the verification toolkit. The contract audit stands: the current contracts let a fresh account mint 100 DPDL for zero work, and one transaction can halt block production. The no-token EU stance holds, covering MiCA Art. 4(3), DAC8 and sanctions screening of payees. The funding stack is the same (EuroHPC Fast Lane, EF ESP, NLnet), with an academic co-author still the highest-leverage "hire". The rental-cost tables and the warning not to trust coding agents with escrow or payout arithmetic still apply, and every phase keeps a kill gate set in advance.

### Build the household tier on open code, and offer partnership early

There are three strategic options, and they can be combined.

The first is to **build an EU household-tier network** on a DiLoCo/SparseLoCo loop. The code would come from Templar's MIT Gauntlet and SparseLoCo implementation ([templar](https://github.com/one-covenant/templar)) and the BSD-3 torchft DiLoCo work ([torchft](https://github.com/meta-pytorch/torchft)). This option targets exactly the gaps Pluralis leaves open: 12–16 GB cards, asymmetric uplinks, outbound-only networking and EU latency. DiLoCo's 15–30 minute rounds are latency-insensitive, so the NA-latency constraint disappears.

The second is to **adopt Pluralis's Apache-2.0 Agora** for a later pipeline "pro tier" of 24–32 GB nodes on fast links, instead of reimplementing it ([agora](https://github.com/PluralisResearch/agora)).

The third is to **offer Pluralis, or IOTA, a partnership early**. AIBC would act as the EU contributor cooperative: legal shell, payouts, identity, verification and community for European members joining their runs. That would differentiate on exactly the layer they have not built, without competing head-on on algorithms.

Option one is the core of the plan, because it is the only one AIBC controls. Option three should be explored in Phase 0, because a "yes" would shortcut years of work.

Team realism shapes every phase. One person at 8–10 hours a week has about 100–130 hours per quarter, by the earlier report's estimate. That covers Phase 0 and, with help, Phase 1. Phase 2 needs paid staff, at least 2–4 full-time equivalents by this report's estimate, funded by grants. Phase 3 needs an institution or a partner.

## Conclusion

The people's datacenter is no longer blocked by algorithms. It is blocked by institutions. The 2026 record shows that idle consumer-class GPUs will turn up for nothing, 612 clients for 60 Pluralis slots, and that bandwidth-light training works. It also shows that every well-funded team chose corporate value capture or token emissions over giving contributors a real claim. That makes the open space unusually suited to a small French team. The scarce assets are a credible cooperative structure, a verified euro payout pipeline, a defined inference right and an EU-latency household tier. None of these needs a GPU cluster, and all of them are expensive for a crypto-native or US venture-backed competitor to copy.

The economics also turn the usual framing around. Paid fairly, contributors' FLOPs cost about as much as rented H100s. The network therefore earns its existence by producing something a cloud bill cannot: a model shaped and governed by its members, released openly and sized to run on their own machines. That argues for designing the inference envelope, meaning active parameters, QAT and nested tiers, before the training run rather than after. It also argues for treating the first 1.5B model as infrastructure proof rather than a product. The most valuable early experiment is not a big run but a small one: whether calibrated replay can tell honest from lazy rounds across a 3090, a 4090 and a 5090. If it can, verified pay for untrusted households becomes practical, and AIBC's original "proof of deep learning" idea finally has the verification step it always lacked.
